## NARRATION (spoken, no on-screen chips)
- object: This robe was made in Japan around the 1660s; today it is in New York, at the Metropolitan Museum of Art. Keep it in view: it travels with us.
- question: Before we look closer, one question to hold on to. We settle it at the very end.
- bolt: Before it is a garment, a kimono is a roll of cloth, handled as one unit called a tan. Its width is the measure that every other part follows.
- cut: Because the cloth is only divided, the seams are few and straight. Traditionally they are sewn by hand, so that the stitches do not show.
- parts: Laid flat, the two fronts overlap by about a third of the body's width. The fit comes later, in the way the robe is worn.
- year: The seasonal change of wardrobe has a name, koromogae, and the whole collection is aired at once, a custom called mushi boshi. Unlined summer robes often carry autumn plants, such as the bellflower.
- pattern: Few early kosode survive, because kosode were worn until they were used up. So scholars read the paintings, and the order books of the drapers, instead.
- kitsuke: After the war, kimono academies spread across Japan and began to issue certificates. In the magazines, the expert does the dressing; the model stands still.
- left: The rule goes back to the Yoro clothing code of 718, which followed Chinese fashion. In the West, kimono were often worn the other way round, and without an obi.
- body: Until about a hundred years ago, cords and obi were tied low, at the hip bones. And the drum knot most people picture was not born until the late Edo period.
- ladder: In late Edo formal dress, the older the wearer, the quieter the pattern. Today the furisode is the Japanese garment a woman is most likely to buy in her lifetime.
- timeline: As court dress was simplified, the outer robes came off and the kosode rose to the surface. In the Meiji period, meeting Western dress turned it into a national costume.
- laws: The rules were proclaimed again and again. Peasants, for one, were to wear plain dark blue, everywhere in the country.
- meisen: Meisen's picture-like patterns drew on Art Nouveau and Art Deco. Only after the war did kimono lose its link with fashion and fun.
- abroad: In England those robes were called banyans; later, Western women found in kimono an ease and a liberation. Poiret described his kimono coat as ample and square-cut.
- hanfu: Further south, Okinawa's bingata once dressed royal and gentry women; since 1972 it has circulated as Japanese kimono and obi.
- answer: The cloth is a flat T, cut from one bolt. Everything else, the season, the rank, the fold and the knot, is in how it is worn; and, as Becke records, rules can be bended.
- end: Every claim on these slides is sourced, and the full exhibition is one click away. Thank you for looking.
