Is a kimono a garment, or a set of rules?

Pick one and hold your answer. It is settled in the last room, not before.

Scroll slowly

Room 1 · Structure

From one bolt to a flat T

Before it is a garment, it is a roll of cloth. Watch it unroll, divide into eight pieces and come together again as a flat T.

  1. 1The bolt

    One bolt, one kimono 反物

    A bolt is about 36–38 cm wide and about 12 m long, and one bolt makes one kimono [7] p.76.

    More

    In the old units: 9 sun 5 bu to 9 sun 8 bu wide, 3 jō or more long. One shaku, about 38 cm, is 10 sun [7] p.80.

  2. 2The width

    Everything follows the width

    The proportions of every part of a kimono derive from the width of the bolt [8] p.50.

    More

    A sleeve, for example, is as wide as the shoulder, or up to one sun (about 3.8 cm) wider [8] p.49 [7] p.80.

  3. 3The cut

    Eight rectangles

    Two body panels, two sleeves, two okumi, a collar and an over-collar: eight rectangles [7] p.76.

    More

    No pattern is drafted; the cut only divides [7] p.80. Sleeves and body panels first, the rest split for okumi and collar, as Ōtsuka Sueko put it in 1972 [7] pp.79-80.

  4. 4Nothing left

    The whole bolt is used

    The bolt is cut and used entire, with nothing left over [7] p.76.

    More

    Becke, quoting Dalby, gives the first mark of a kimono as [A] geometrical use of standard fabric widths sewn with minimal cutting [4] p.10.

  5. 5The seams

    Straight seams, sewn by hand 手縫い

    Traditionally a kimono is sewn by hand, without large stitches showing on the face [7] p.76.

    More

    The seams have names: the back seam, senui, and the side seams, wakinui [7] p.77.

  6. 6The T

    No darts, no tucks

    In its unworn form, a kimono has a completely flat surface. [4] p.10

    More

    Becke again: no tucks or darts are added to the fabric which would provide the garment with a three-dimensional shape [4] p.10.

Eight rectangles, and not one dart.

Room 2 · Structure

The front, the fit, the re-making

Turn to the front of the T. Each piece has a name, the fit is a matter of measurement, and the whole robe could be taken apart again.

  1. 1The front edge

    Okumi and collar 衽・衿

    Each front carries an extra panel, the okumi [7] p.76. The collar is an attached neckband sewn around the front opening [4] p.10.

    More

    The eri is simply cut out in long band [22] p.528; the kakeeri, the over-collar, is the eighth piece [7] p.76. Standard okumi: 15 cm [8] p.49.

  2. 2Fitted to her

    As long as she is tall 身丈・裄

    Mitake, the body length, equals the wearer's height; yuki, back centre to sleeve opening, is fitted to her build [7] p.76.

    More

    For a woman 160 cm tall: mitake 159.6, yuki 66.5, sleeve 49.4 cm [7] p.76. Laid flat, a third of the body width overlaps [8] p.49.

  3. 3Re-making

    Unpick it, and it is cloth again 繰り回し

    Kimono were remade, kurimawashi, as seasons, uses and wearers changed, and as cloth wore out [7] p.75.

    More

    For washing, silk was unpicked and joined back into a bolt [7] p.76. Re-making, the authors conclude, was built into the form [7] p.80.

Room 3 · Fabric and season

Warmth is woven, not cut

One shape all year. What suits a robe to cold or warm months is the weave and the lining, not the cut [6] p.20.

Colour, age, marriage

In late-Edo women's formal dress, the older the wearer, the more restrained the pattern [29] p.25. Today the long-sleeved furisode is mainly reserved for unmarried women [4] p.53.

Becke's young interviewees expressed themselves through colour more than pattern [4] p.134 [4] p.137.

Room 5 · Kitsuke

Kitsuke: putting it on

A flat robe gets its final fit and shape only in the dressing, called kitsuke [4] p.11.

  1. 1Before the layers

    Taught and certified 着付け

    Kimono academies spread across Japan after the war [4] p.77, issuing certificates after the completion of certain courses [4] p.78.

    More

    In a 2006 survey of 128 women students at one university, 8.6 per cent could put on a kimono themselves [27] p.774.

  2. 2Juban, then kimono

    Left over right

    Furisode magazines begin with the under-kimono and end with the obi knot [4] p.72. The robe is always draped from the left side over to the right [4] p.69.

    More

    The other way round is now associated with death and the afterlife [4] p.70.

  3. 3The tuck

    Ohashori おはしょり

    The extra length becomes the fold of fabric beneath the obi [4] p.71.

    More

    In a yukata study of nine young women, the fold slipped less when the cord was tied at the top of the hip bone than at the waist [19] p.81.

  4. 4The obi

    Where the pressure falls 帯

    In a study of nine young women, clothing pressure was highest at the obi's upper edge, near the armpit [21] p.53.

    More

    Of 333 graduating students in kimono, about eight in ten felt it two to three times as constricting as Western clothes [21] p.56.

  5. 5Last

    Cord, sash and knot

    Magazines set the obi-jime in the middle of the obi, add an obi-age to complete the ensemble [4] p.71, and tie the knot facing the wearer's back [4] p.71.

Room 6 · Body language

The body in the robe

From behind: the knot, the collar, the hem. Then, on the wall, prints of robes being worn and put on.

  1. 1The knot

    Younger than it looks

    The taiko or drum knot, a furisode magazine notes, was not born until the late Edo period [4] p.110.

  2. 2The nape

    Collar set back from the neck

    In a 1976 study of three women, the best collar set-back was 3 to 5 cm [22] p.530.

    More

    Standard furisode dressing aims to hide potentially arousing areas of the body, such as the nape [4] p.165. Paris around 1907 preferred to reveal it [5] p.8.

  3. 3The hem

    Level, just above the feet

    In magazines the hem is set straight and always ends just above the models' feet [4] p.70.

    More

    In a gait study of three women, a level hem gave longer strides than one raised at the front corners [23] p.695.

  4. 4Movement

    Walking, sitting, stairs

    Furisode magazines teach how to appropriately move one's body when walking and sitting down [4] pp.79-80.

    More

    The result, Becke argues, is constant monitoring and correcting of one's body [4] p.80.

  5. 5Posture

    Where the obi sits

    In a lab study of eight young women, the low obi position used by men helped in stabilizing the body trunk, straightening posture [18] p.126.

    More

    Women's obi today sit higher, from under the bust to the waist [20] p.23.

Room 7 · The kimono family

One shape, many relatives

Over-robe, jacket, trousers, under-robe: one family. In kimono, design signals the occasion and season, and the wearer's age and standing [4] p.11.

Ranked, top to bottom

The usual order, as a 2022 study of the kimono trade states it [25] p.16. Tsumugi, between komon and yukata there, is left out: no source here defines it.

A ladder for men

By the mid-Edo period a black crested haori was the most formal; plain, then komon, then stripes were each less formal [37] P-386.

Since the kamishimo was abolished after the Meiji Restoration, haori and hakama have been men's formal dress [37] P-386.

Room 8 · Four centuries

From under-layer to national dress

It began as an under-layer [30] p.516. Then came laws, drapers, prints, a new name and, after the war, a rulebook [4] p.75.

Laws on cloth

The Tokugawa government issued sumptuary laws on the type of clothes members of each class were allowed to wear [4] p.99: 132 times between 1615 and 1866 [39] p.432.

Their purpose, in Nishimura's summary: to maintain the class hierarchy [39] p.432.

Meisen, the everyday silk

Meisen is a plain-weave silk of pre-dyed yarn [14] p.1. From Taishō into post-war Shōwa it was the mass-market kimono [15] p.37, its picture-like patterns influenced by Art Nouveau and Art Deco [15] p.37. Output peaked in 1930 [14] p.3.

Room 9 · Relatives and influence

Relatives and influence

Robes that left Japan, and robes from its neighbours. Each comparison on this wall is one a scholar made.

What the West took

Fukai's verdict: The most important point was the rectangular cut that followed the warp and weft of the fabric and the unconfined garment that resulted from it. [5] p.8

In 1906 Poiret freed the silhouette from the corset [5] p.8.

Neighbours: hanfu

A 2024 study of Chinese hanfu finds that the cross-shaped structure of Hanfu did not change [40] p.39. Its front closes left over right, as Japanese dress does [40] p.44.

How to read the numbers

Every statement in this exhibition, on the walls and in the audio guide, carries a source number and a page. Words in quotation marks are the source’s own. Many sources are in Japanese; where they are, the English on screen is a paraphrase. The full ledger, one row per claim with the passage it rests on, is public: claims ledger.

Object titles, dates and materials are the museums’ own records. They are cited as records, not as scholarship.
