Start with gifts. When the Dutch came to eh-doh, the shogun returned their presents with silk robes. In Holland they became men's house gowns, light and warm. One writer sees them on Vermeer's astronomer and geographer.

Two centuries later, Western women took the kimono up, but loosely. They wore it without an oh-bee, and often right over left: in Japan, the way the dead are dressed.

In London, a Japanese village opened in Knightsbridge in January eighteen eighty-five. Gilbert is said to have learned from its people when he staged The Mikado.

Nearer Japan, the comparisons turn to cloth and climate. In Korea, one lecture notes, the full skirt caught the warmth rising from heated floors.

Ryoo-kyoo-an bin-gah-tah absorbed Javanese and Indian chintz and Chinese printed cloth. Since nineteen seventy-two it has been sold as Japanese kimono and oh-bee.

The Eye-noo word aht-toos means cloth from the inner bark of trees such as elm and linden. Now turn round. The robe on its rack waits at the far end of the hall.
