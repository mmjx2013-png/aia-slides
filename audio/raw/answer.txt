Walk back down the hall now. Past the prints, past the robes, to the flat robe on its rack.

Becker's thesis follows the foo-ree-soh-deh at the coming-of-age day. But her definition is a general one.

The shape is the plain part. Unworn, a kimono is completely flat. No darts, no tucks, nothing to hold a body's form.

The practice is the rest. The head of an old kimono house, Yah-mah-goo-chee Ghen-bay, put a figure on it: seventy percent of what makes a kimono a kimono is how it is worn. That is his view, not a measurement.

And the practice has a history. Laws on cloth under the shoguns. After the war, one book with three hundred and ninety rules.

So the answer is both. A garment, and a set of rules. Whichever you chose at the entrance, you were holding half of it.

Becker adds one more thing. Rules can be bent, she writes, and clothes altered.

The robe stays on its rack, flat, until someone puts it on. Thank you for walking with us.
