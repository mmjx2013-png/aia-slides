As you walk towards the figure, ask who teaches this. Becke traces a wave of books in the nineteen-seventies on the correct way to wear kimono. One large school opened in 1964 and, Becke notes, runs five academies in Japan's biggest cities.

The first layer goes on. In one Japanese case study, a professional dressing of a foo-ree-soh-deh used sixteen separate items: padding, under-layers, cords, boards, an oh-bee pillow. Together they weighed about three kilograms, nearly four times a sweater and jeans.

Left over right is old. Becke traces it to a clothing code of the year 718, which followed Chinese fashion.

The fold at the waist is easily disturbed. In the yoo-kah-tah study, a bow made kneeling on the floor shifted it most; a standing bow, least.

Women today tie the oh-bee high, from under the bust to the waist. About a century ago, cords and oh-bee sat low, at the hip bones.

The knot is tied last, at the back. Walk round and look.
