Begin with the long sleeves. In the late eh-doh period, the writer Kee-tah-gah-wah Moh-ree-sah-dah sorted the robes of his day into four grades, from formal down to everyday. For a woman's formal dress, the older the wearer, the quieter the pattern.

One study of a kimono magazine calls the long-sleeved robe the kimono a Japanese woman today is most likely ever to buy.

Sleeves could also be cut down. In wartime, brides wore a black foo-ree-soh-deh with a train, because its sleeves could later be shortened to make a toh-meh-soh-deh, a married woman's formal robe.

A survey in one Toh-toh-ree city, in the late nineteen-sixties, counted two hundred and six different crests.

The jacket is the easiest member to borrow. In Kyoto's flea markets, visitors take to the hah-oh-ree because it needs no oh-bee and no wrapping.
