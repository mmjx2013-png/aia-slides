Start with the small repeat, then walk on to the large designs. Guides call the large, continuous kind ceremonial. It treats the whole robe as one picture, and the motifs gather below the oh-bee and at the left shoulder.

Robes of this age are rare. Koy-deh explains why: the koh-soh-deh was a thing to be worn out. So she turned to paintings, and counted nearly a thousand robes in them.

Her finding is that no style stood alone. Each borrowed elements from the others, and in exchange made the next.

Koy-deh ties the kahn-boon style to a wider public. As the shogun's order settled, townspeople came to enjoy the koh-soh-deh, in quality and in number.

Among the young women Becker interviewed, the code was read less closely. Asked about the flowers on her own foo-ree-soh-deh, one told her: flowers are flowers, right? Colour mattered more to them. In the next room, the flat picture meets a body.
