# LMT (Lockheed Martin Corporation): paper research memo, 2026-11-27

Source: the inputs file only (10-K 0001628280-26-004195, filed 2026-01-29, FY ending 2025-12-31; XBRL screen; price pack; labelled headlines). The MD&A excerpt in the file is only the section heading, so no MD&A figures were available. Anything not in the file is marked "not in the filing excerpt".

## Verdict
**WATCH.** A high-quality, cash-generative defence prime, but at about 20x EV/EBIT (20% above its own history) and a 5% FCF yield after a +36% one-year run, the price already pays for the good news, and concentration in one customer and one program is a real risk.

## Business
- Lockheed Martin is a US defence prime contractor (SIC 37, guided missiles and space vehicles). It sells weapons systems, aircraft, missiles, space systems and sustainment services.
- Customers: 72% of 2025 sales came from the US Government, including 63% from the Department of War (DoW, formerly DoD) (10-K 0001628280-26-004195, Item 1A). The remainder is mainly international and foreign military sales (FMS), including F-35 partner countries. A split of the remaining 28% is not in the filing excerpt.
- Largest program: the F-35, 27% of 2025 consolidated sales (10-K, Item 1A). It spans development, production and sustainment contracts.
- Growth areas named in the risk factors: hypersonics, classified programs and next-generation franchise programs (10-K, Item 1A).
- Segment names and segment revenue/margins are not in the filing excerpt.

## Moat
Evidence for:
- Incumbency on long-lived programs. The 10-K itself says it expects to "compete and be well positioned as the incumbent on existing programs".
- Very high barriers: security clearances, classified work, certification, and multi-decade sustainment tails on the F-35.
- Returns: ROIC 22.9% (XBRL screen), above a typical industrial cost of capital. ROE of 74.7% is inflated by buybacks and a small equity base, so I do not rely on it.
- FCF margin is stable: average 8.8%, standard deviation 0.8 points (XBRL screen).

Evidence against:
- The customer is a monopsony. It can terminate for convenience, unilaterally definitize undefinitized contracts, protest or re-compete, and push fixed-price terms (10-K, Item 1A).
- New contract-structure risk: a recent Executive Order could restrict dividends, buybacks and executive pay for contractors that miss performance requirements. The 10-K says it would apply only after DoW finds a contractor failed performance requirements, and that it expects it to be implemented through a DFARS clause (10-K, Item 1A). This directly threatens the buyback model.
- Rising competition from startups and non-traditional entrants, and from vertical integration among rivals (10-K, Item 1A).
- Pricing is capped by cost-plus and negotiated margins. The moat protects the franchise, but it does not give the pricing power seen in commercial franchises.

Net: a real, durable position, but it is a regulated-customer moat with limited margin upside.

## Why is it cheap?
It is not clearly cheap. On the screen, valuation scores 0.494 (mid-pack), EV/EBIT is 1.195x its own history (about 20% above average), and the stock is up 36% over 1 year and 46% over 3 years. What the market worries about, per the 10-K:
1. Budget risk: continuing resolutions, shutdowns, debt-ceiling fights and reprioritization can delay or cut funding (10-K, Item 1A).
2. F-35 concentration: 27% of sales rests on one program that faces recurring cost, schedule, software and sustainment scrutiny, and potential next-generation competition.
3. Capital-return policy: the Executive Order on buybacks and dividends. A buyback-led story ("buyback machine", per a Seeking Alpha headline) would lose its main support if the clause applies.
4. Programme execution: fixed-price development risk. Specific charges are not in the filing excerpt.

My view: the budget and F-35 worries are real but slow-moving and well understood. The buyback-restriction worry is more likely to be over- than under-stated, because the 10-K says it applies only after a performance failure. But the shares are not discounted for any of this: at a 5% FCF yield the margin of safety is thin. The "cheapness" is relative to the market's valuation of defence as a theme, not absolute.

## Disruption risk
Not a software, payments or internet name. The relevant disruption is from non-traditional entrants (startups, commercial tech firms) and cheaper, faster, lower-capability systems (10-K, Item 1A), plus possible next-generation platforms competing with the F-35. Medium and slow-moving.

## Risks
Top five, most important first, from Item 1A:
1. **US Government dependence and budget timing.** 72% of sales are to the US Government (63% to DoW). Shutdowns, continuing resolutions (which restrict new starts) and partially funded contracts can delay orders and cash, and can leave unfunded costs unreimbursed.
2. **F-35 concentration.** 27% of sales. Cuts or delays by the US, partners or FMS customers, plus performance, supplier, software, sustainment-cost and next-generation competition risk, would hit revenue and cash directly.
3. **Procurement reform and shareholder-return limits.** Executive Orders overhaul the FAR and acquisition process, move toward multi-year structures, and may restrict dividends, buybacks and executive pay for contractors that miss performance requirements. Applicability and impact are described as uncertain.
4. **Contract-terms risk.** Termination for convenience or default, unilateral definitization of undefinitized contract actions, and bid protests can reduce profit, delay cash and cost the company awards.
5. **Competition and pricing.** New entrants, non-traditional contractors, vertical integration among rivals, and a shift to multi-award, competitively bid contracts create pricing pressure and higher bid costs.

The excerpt of Item 1A is cut off at 14,000 characters, so further risks (suppliers, cyber, pensions, litigation, international) are not in the filing excerpt, although the 10-K cross-references supplier performance as a separate risk.

## Valuation
- FCF yield 4.99%; normalized 4.77% (XBRL screen). That is below a typical 6-8% hurdle for a cash-account value holding.
- EV/EBIT 20.5x; normalized 18.6x; 1.195x its own history (XBRL screen). The stock trades at a premium to its own past.
- What is priced in: roughly mid-single-digit growth continuing (revenue and FCF CAGR about 4%), stable margins, a continued F-35 production and sustainment stream, and a buyback programme that is not materially restricted. With a 5% yield and 4% growth, the implied total return is about 9%, before any multiple change. If the multiple reverted to its own history (about 1/1.195, or -16%), that would offset about two years of earnings and cash growth.
- Price: $599.24 close on 2026-11-25. 1m -4%, 6m +13%, 1y +36%, 3y +46%. Volatility 31% a year. Drawdown from the 5-year high is -10%; worst 5-year drawdown was -32% (price pack). A -32% drawdown on an 8% position is a -2.6% portfolio hit, which is acceptable, but the entry should not be chased.
- The 12-1 momentum factor is 0.417, and s_momentum is 0.789, so the trend is positive.

## Thesis
Only if the stock is bought at a better price:
- A franchise with 20%+ ROIC, locked-in incumbency on a decades-long F-35 and missile stream, and steady 9% FCF margins.
- Defence budgets and multi-year procurement shifts (longer-term contract structures, per the 10-K) can lengthen visibility.
- A shrinking share count (-11.75%) compounds per-share FCF faster than the roughly 4% business growth, if buybacks continue.
- Missile, munitions and hypersonics demand is a growth leg beyond the F-35 (named in the 10-K; size not in the filing excerpt).
- At about a 5% FCF yield plus growth, the shares are a fair-value compounder, not a bargain. The case improves at an FCF yield of 6% or more (a price of roughly $500 or below, all else equal).

## What breaks the thesis
Checkable signals:
1. The DFARS clause implementing the buyback/dividend Executive Order is published, and DoW determines LMT failed a performance requirement, so restrictions apply.
2. F-35 falls below about 25% of sales through lower deliveries or funding cuts, or a partner nation cuts its planned order (checkable in the next 10-K and 10-Qs).
3. Program charges or reach-forward losses on a fixed-price development program in any quarter (size of past charges not in the filing excerpt).
4. FCF margin below about 8% for two consecutive annual periods (history shows 8.8% average with 0.8-point standard deviation).
5. Net debt/FCF rising above 3.5x (now 2.89x), or buybacks cut materially in the next 10-K.
