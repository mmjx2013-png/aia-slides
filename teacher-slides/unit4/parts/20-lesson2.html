Lesson 2 · Primary and secondary data

Who collected the data?

Primary data

You collect it yourself

  • Ask friends their favourite snack
  • Measure the height of classmates
  • Count cars at the school gate
Secondary data

Someone else already collected it

  • School records: who was absent
  • A weather website: Bangkok rain
  • A library book about Thailand
  • The news: BTS passengers
Ask: who collected it? You did → primary. Someone else did → secondary.
Start with the two headings. Primary data: you collect it yourself, first-hand, for your own question. Secondary data: someone else collected it before, and you use it. Read the primary examples (asking friends, measuring heights, counting cars), then the secondary ones (school records, a website, a book, the news). Ask students for one more example of each. Ask: "Which kind is faster to get? Which kind fits your question better?" (Secondary is faster; primary fits your own question exactly.) Beat 1: the test question: who collected it?

Four ways to collect data

1

e.g. Ask the canteen cook which dish sells best.

e.g. Students in 1/2 fill in an online form about sleep.
3

Observe or measure

Watch, count or measure
e.g. Measure the rain in the school garden each day.
4

Experiment

Change one thing, compare
e.g. Grow one bean plant in sun, one in shade.
Four ways to collect primary data. Name each card, then click (beat 1) for the examples. 1 Interview: you talk and ask questions, face to face. 2 Questionnaire: everyone answers the same written questions, on paper or in an online form. 3 Observation or measurement: you watch, count or measure (a tally of cars, rain in a rain gauge, height with a ruler). 4 Experiment: you change one thing and compare the results (sun vs shade, measure both plants each day). Ask: "To find how long students in 1/2 sleep, which way is best?" (A questionnaire: fast for a whole class.)
Lesson 2 · Population and sample

Census or survey?

Populationall 30 students in class 1/2
Census · ask 30 of 30
Sample Survey · ask 6 of 30
Population
The whole group we want to learn about.
Census
Collect data from every member of the population.
Sample · Survey
A sample is a part of the population. A survey collects data from a sample only.
Our question: "What snack do students in 1/2 like best?" The population is the whole group we want to know about: all 30 students in 1/2. Beat 1, census: we ask every one of the 30 students (all icons turn blue). Beat 2, survey: we ask only a sample, 6 of the 30 (the coral icons move down into the sample box). 6 out of 30 = 6/30 = 1/5 = 20% of the class. Say: "Pick the sample fairly, for example by drawing names from a box, so every student has the same chance."

Census

Small group, or must be exact → census. Big group, little time or money → survey.
Compare the bars. A census asks everyone, so it takes more time and costs more, but the result is exact. A survey asks only a sample: quicker and cheaper, and the result is close (an estimate) if the sample is chosen fairly. Then the two real examples. Census: Thailand's National Statistical Office runs a population and housing census about every 10 years and tries to count every person. Survey: the canteen asks 100 of 2,400 students; 100 ÷ 2,400 = 1/24, about 4% of the school. Asking all 2,400 would take far too long. Beat 1: the rule of thumb. Ask: "Which would you use to find the favourite fruit of everyone in Thailand?" (Survey.)

ACensus

One student per case comes to the board, taps Census or Survey, and explains why (think about time, cost and how exact). Then click to reveal, one case per beat. Case 1: survey. 2,400 students is a big population and there is only a few days; a fair sample gives a good estimate. For example, if the school has 60 classes of 40 students (60 × 40 = 2,400), pick 2 students at random from each class: 60 × 2 = 120 students, which is 120 ÷ 2,400 = 5% of the school. Case 2: census. Each of the 32 students needs a shirt that fits, so we must ask every student; an estimate is not good enough, and 32 people is a small group. Case 3: survey. Tasting a bag opens it, so testing all 5,000 bags would leave nothing to sell. The factory tests a sample.

Exercise 2 · Task 4 · Live class survey

How do you come to school?

  1. Come up and tap above your way.
  2. One dot for each student.
Everyone in our class answers, so it is a census of our class.
Live survey. Each student comes to the board and taps one dot above their usual way to school (use the main way if they use two). Count the dots together: the total should equal the number of students here today. Then ask the question in the box; a student writes an answer with the pen. Beat 1 shows the answer: every student in our class answers, so it is a census of our class (population = our class). If someone is absent, we did not ask everyone, so strictly it is a survey of the class. Extension: "If we use these dots to describe the whole school, what is our class?" (A sample, so for the school it is a survey; ask whether one class is a fair sample of the whole school.)
