Say: "We asked 24 students in 1/3: how many brothers and sisters do you have?" Show the raw slips. Ask: "Which answer is the most common?" Students find it hard: that is why we present data. Click (beat 1): the slips sort themselves into groups and the table counts them. 0 siblings: 5 · 1 sibling: 10 · 2 siblings: 6 · 3 siblings: 3. Check: 5 + 10 + 6 + 3 = 24 ✓ (and again: 10 + 6 = 16, 16 + 5 = 21, 21 + 3 = 24 ✓). Click (beat 2): each pile becomes a bar. Ask: "What do you see now?" Most students have 1 sibling (10 students); the fewest have 3 (3 students). A table gives exact numbers; a chart shows the pattern fast.

Ask: "What 3 parts can you see?" Let students point. Beat 1: the title says WHAT (mango sticky rice sold), WHERE (the school canteen) and WHEN (16–20 November 2026, Monday to Friday). Beat 2: the table: rows and columns, with a Total row. Check the total: 42 + 35 + 48 + 30 + 55 = 210 (42 + 35 = 77, 77 + 48 = 125, 125 + 30 = 155, 155 + 55 = 210 ✓). Beat 3: the source tells us where the data came from, so we can trust it or check it. Quick question: "Which day sold the most?" (Friday, 55 boxes).
Lesson 3 · Two-way tables

Read a two-way table

A two-way table sorts data in two ways: by class and by boys or girls.

  1. Find the row and the column. They meet at one cell.
  2. Add across to get each row total.
  3. Add down to get each column total.
Check: 10 + 12 + 14 = 36 = 19 + 17

A two-way table sorts the same students in two ways: by class (rows) and by boys or girls (columns). Beat 1: "How many girls in 1/2 ride the bus?" A pictogram uses pictures to show data. Beat 1: the title tells us what it shows. Beat 2: the labels: one row for each day. Beat 3: the key: one cup stands for 10 cups. Every icon is the same size and lined up, so rows are easy to compare. Beat 4: a half icon means half of 10 = 5 cups. Read each row: Mon 3 × 10 = 30 · Tue 4½ × 10 = 45 · Wed 4 × 10 = 40 · Thu 2½ × 10 = 25 · Fri 6 × 10 = 60. Total 30 + 45 + 40 + 25 + 60 = 200 cups (20 icons × 10 = 200 ✓). Ask: "Why not draw 200 little cups?" (too many; the key makes it short).
Lesson 3 · Bar charts

The parts of a bar chart

A bar chart uses bars to compare groups. A good one has:

  1. A title and axis labels
  2. A scale with equal steps (here 2)
  3. Bars of the same width
  4. Equal gaps between bars
A bar chart compares groups with bars. The taller the bar, the more students. Beat 1: the title and the two axis labels (Way to school, Number of students) say what is shown. Beat 2: the scale goes up in equal steps of 2: 0, 2, 4 … 14. Beat 3: every bar has the same width, so only the height matters. Beat 4: the gaps between bars are equal too. Read the chart: School van 12, Car 9, BTS 7, Motorbike 5, Walk 3. Total 12 + 9 + 7 + 5 + 3 = 36 students (12 + 9 = 21, 21 + 7 = 28, 28 + 5 = 33, 33 + 3 = 36 ✓). Ask: "How many more come by school van than by car?" (12 − 9 = 3).

Key: 1 book = 20 books, so half a book = 10 books. Let students tap a choice (or come to the board) before you reveal. Beat 1: Q1 Tuesday has 4½ icons: 4 × 20 = 80, plus half of 20 = 10, so 90 books (C). Wrong answers: 4.5 counts icons but forgets the key; 80 forgets the half icon; 100 counts the half as a whole. Exercise 3A · Double bar charts

Read a double bar chart

A double bar chart shows two groups side by side. The legend tells us blue = boys and coral = girls. Students type each answer (or come to the board and point with the pen), then click: each answer appears ON the chart, with a line to the scale. Beat 1: girls who chose badminton = 13 (the coral bar stops halfway between 12 and 14). Beat 2: football boys 16, girls 7, so 16 − 7 = 9 more boys. Beat 3: volleyball boys 5 + girls 12 = 17 students. Whole chart: boys 16 + 9 + 5 + 10 = 40, girls 7 + 13 + 12 + 4 = 36, total 76 students. Extra question: "Which sport do the girls like most?" (badminton, 13).
