Four places where data helps us decide. First show only the data in each card and ask: "What would you decide with this data?" Take two or three ideas for each card. Beat 1 shows one possible decision for each card. Canteen: if we know what sells each day, the cook makes more of the favourites and throws away less food. Sports day: with everyone's 50 m running time we can make teams that are fair, with fast and slow runners in every team. Weather: rainfall records from past years show which months are dry, so sports day goes in a dry month. Sleep: if many students sleep too little, they can choose an earlier bedtime on school nights. Key message: data turns a guess into a better decision.

A case study that follows the four steps (the tracker at the top lights up). Step 1 Ask: the canteen can add only one new drink. Question: "Which new drink would students buy most?" This is a statistical question because the answers vary. Beat 1, Collect: we asked 120 students, 20 from each grade M1 to M6 (6 × 20 = 120), so every grade has a voice. Votes: Thai tea 42, Mango smoothie 36, Coconut water 24, Butterfly pea juice 18. Check the total: 42 + 36 + 24 + 18 = 120. Beat 2, Present: a bar chart makes the biggest group easy to see (scale: each grid line is 10 votes). Beat 3, Decide: Thai tea has the tallest bar, so add Thai tea. 42 ÷ 120 = 0.35 = 35%. Check: 35% of 120 = 0.35 × 120 = 42. Ask: "Mango smoothie is close (36). What could the canteen do next term?" (try it later, or ask again).

Task 1. Shop B's poster says "We sell twice as much as Shop A!" and its bar for Shop B looks twice as tall. Let pairs answer the question (tap a choice) and come to the board before you reveal anything. Beat 1 (hint): look at the y axis. It starts at 90, not 0, so the bars only show the part above 90: Shop A 94 − 90 = 4, Shop B 98 − 90 = 8. That is why B looks twice as tall. Beat 2: the honest chart starts at 0. The bars are 94 and 98, almost the same height. Students can first sketch it with the pen on the dashed card. Beat 3, answer B: 98 − 94 = 4 more boxes. 4 ÷ 94 ≈ 0.043, about 4% more, not 100% more (twice as many would be 2 × 94 = 188 boxes). Choice C (8) comes from reading the bar heights above 90. Rule: always read the axis before you believe a chart.

Unit summary, one lesson per click. Ask the class to say each idea before you click. Lesson 1: a statistical question expects answers that vary, so we collect data (example: "How long do 1/2 students sleep?"). Lesson 2: primary data we collect ourselves; secondary data was collected by someone else (books, websites). A census collects data from everyone in the population; a survey collects data from a sample. Lesson 3: we present data in a table, pictogram, bar chart, line graph or pie chart, and choose the one that fits (line graph for change over time, pie chart for parts of a whole). Lesson 4: the data cycle (ask, collect, present, interpret and decide), and always check the axis before you trust a chart.

Unit review, calculations. Data: how the 40 students of 1/2 get to school. Check the total: 14 + 10 + 8 + 6 + 2 = 40. Give time to work, then invite two students to the board before you click. Q1 (beat 1): percent = part ÷ total × 100 = 14 ÷ 40 × 100 = 0.35 × 100 = 35%. Check: 35% of 40 = 0.35 × 40 = 14. Q2 (beat 2): angle = part ÷ total × 360° = 14 ÷ 40 × 360° = 0.35 × 360° = 126°. Check: 126 ÷ 360 = 0.35. The other angles: car 10 ÷ 40 × 360 = 90°, walk 8 ÷ 40 × 360 = 72°, BTS 6 ÷ 40 × 360 = 54°, bicycle 2 ÷ 40 × 360 = 18°. Sum: 126 + 90 + 72 + 54 + 18 = 360°.
