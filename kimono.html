1The garment

Straight pieces from one bolt. Hardly any waste.

People know a kimono by ‘its t-shape form, characteristic sleeves and the use of a wide obi’ (a sash).[4] p.10

The bolt (the roll of cloth) ‘comes in a standardised width’. Cutting gives ‘geometrical pieces with square outlines’, and ‘hardly any wastage’.[4] pp.10–11 Fukai: ‘cut along straight lines and constructed of rectangles’.[5] PDF p.1

The parts, in words that source [4] quotes from the writer Liza Dalby: see the labels.[4] p.10, quoting DalbyThe lines are traced by hand on the photograph and are approximate. The words describe kimono in general; the robe is a kosode of about the 1660s. No source read gives a panel count or Japanese part names, so none are shown.

It has ‘no tucks or darts’. Unworn, it has ‘a completely flat surface’.[4] p.10 So where does the shape come from?

2Fabric and season

First the cloth. Then the calendar.

Against Western ornament, kimono ‘emphasized the material, the fabric of clothing’.[5] PDF p.7

JanFebMarAprMayJunJulAugSepOctNovDec

Awase 袷, lined: ‘appropriate from October until May’.[6] p.20

Hitoe 単衣, unlined: ‘the warm months that spread from June to September’.[6] p.20

Ro 絽 and sha 紗, sheer ‘breathable’ weaves: ‘while the hot and humid days of August linger’.[6] p.20

The wardrobe change is called koromogae.[6] p.20 Patterns run ahead of it: one guide says to wear a flower ‘about one month ahead of the actual blossoming’.[4] p.62, quoting a 2017 guide

A convention, not a law: ‘a guiding principle, not mandatory but strongly encouraged’.[6] p.22 Of her young interviewees, the author of [4] writes: ‘Many young women might not be able to recognise this importance’.[4] p.138

3Pattern

A flat cloth is a canvas. Placement is a code.

Flat cloth is ‘an ideal canvas for dyers, painters and embroiderers’.[4] p.11

Komon 小紋: ‘pervasive and repetitive patterns’ are ‘generally considered less formal’.[4] p.62

Eba-moyō: ‘asymmetrical, continuous’ arrangements ‘are regarded as ceremonial’. Motifs gather ‘below the obi and the left shoulder’.[4] p.62Source [4] reports this from kimono guides and from Dalby. Neither photograph is labelled as one of these arrangements on its Commons file page.

Designs travelled in pattern books, hinagata-bon, which ‘informed consumers of available kimono designs and styles’.[4] p.28

4Dressing

The fit is made by dressing, not by cutting.

着付け kitsukethe dressing process[4] p.11

Flat kimono ‘are fitted to the body as part of the dressing process’. Kitsuke ‘provides the final fit and shape’.[4] p.11

Academies ‘have turned the knowledge and dressing process … into a discipline’, with certificates for finished courses.[4] pp.77–78

  4. obi-jime (obi belt) ‘in the middle of the obi’, and obi-age (obi cloth) ‘to complete the ensemble’[4] p.71

5Movement

Then the rules reach the body.

Magazines about the furisode, the long-sleeved kimono,[4] p.53 teach how to move ‘when walking and sitting down, as well as when climbing up and down stairs’.[4] pp.79–80

Also covered: ‘getting in and out of a car’, a meal, the bathroom. The result is ‘constant monitoring and correcting of one’s body’.[4] p.80

Even the collar depends on who sets the rule. Standard furisode dressing ‘aims to hide … the nape’.[4] p.165 In Paris around 1907, a neckband ‘low in back, revealing the nape’ was ‘the fashionable way to wear kimono’.[5] PDF p.8

The labels on the photograph: how models are posed and dressed in furisode magazines.[4] pp.70, 78These are lessons and images printed for one ceremony: the coming-of-age day, a public holiday ‘on the second Monday of January’ for new adults.[4] p.37 They are not a record of how every kimono wearer moves. The robe shown is the 1660s kosode, not a furisode.

6History

The rules were fixed late.[4] pp.11, 72

Edo period · 1600–1868[4] p.164

Fashion, by order

Robes called kosode were the ‘precursor of the modern kimono’.[5] PDF p.4 Laws set ‘the type of clothes members of each class were allowed to wear’.[4] p.99 Pattern books, hinagata-bon, ‘informed consumers of available kimono designs’.[4] p.28

Meiji period · 1868–1912[4] p.72

A national costume

‘Reframed as Japan’s national costume through an encounter with “Western” forms of dress’.[4] p.10 One shape became ‘frozen in time’.[4] p.72

Post-war decades[4] p.68

A rulebook

Japanese dress was ‘largely abandoned by the majority’.[4] p.68 Wearing it became ‘vigorously standardised’.[4] p.11 A 1972 book lists ‘390 rules’.[4] p.75

1990s to 2000s[4] pp.14, 118

Rented, revived

‘Rental options gained popularity’.[4] p.118 Then came ‘an increased demand and appreciation of vintage kimono’.[4] p.14

7A test

Take the rules away. Or turn them up.

EuropeWithout the rules

A robe of ease

Well before the late 1800s, kimono ‘had made their way to Holland and become popular as men’s domestic garments’.[5] PDF p.2

Western women saw in kimono ‘an ease and a liberation’ and ‘adopted them as dressing gowns’.[5] PDF p.4

Fashion found in the kimono a structure for ‘liberation from the corset’.[5] PDF p.11

One essay on fashion history. It describes some Western wearers and designers, not all.

JapanWith every rule

A robe of form

A magazine calls the furisode ‘the most formal garment for unmarried women’.[4] p.53

Its dressing instructions are ‘communicated as fixed and non-negotiable’.[4] p.72

The rules make it ‘arguably very similar to a school uniform’.[4] p.123

One garment type at one ceremony, not all kimono. The author adds: ‘Rules can be bended’ (sic).[4] p.123

One garment. Two meanings.this deck’s reading of [5] PDF p.4 and [4] p.123

The answerSlide 2, settled

A garment,
or a set of rules?

Front: the garment, with ‘a completely flat surface’, cut in straight pieces.[4] p.10 · [5] PDF p.1

Now turn it over.

Both.

The back of the kimono lists the rules: which occasion; which season; where the pattern sits; left side over right; first the under-robe; last the obi knot; how to walk and sit; how to climb stairs.

Becke, the author of [4], defines kimono by ‘the garment’s shape and practice of fitting it to the body’.[4] p.10 (Bold added.)

‘Seventy percent of what makes a kimono a kimono is how it is worn.’[4] p.11, quoting the head of a kimono house

It takes both. The garment is simple; the rules of dressing and formality give it meaning: ‘social and cultural aspects and contexts strongly determine how the garment should be worn’.[4] p.11Most lines on the back are rules [4] records for the furisode at the coming-of-age day (pp.69–80); the season and pattern lines come from kimono guides (p.62). Her definition of kimono in general rests on shape and fitting (pp.10–11).
