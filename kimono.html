1The garment

Straight pieces from one bolt. Hardly any waste.

People know a kimono by ‘its t-shape form, characteristic sleeves and the use of a wide obi’ (a sash).[4] p.10

The bolt ‘comes in a standardised width’. Cutting gives ‘geometrical pieces with square outlines’, and ‘hardly any wastage’.[4] pp.10–11

The parts, in the words Becke quotes from Dalby: see the labels.[4] p.10, quoting DalbyThe drawing is a schematic. No source read for this deck gives a panel count or Japanese part names, so none are shown.

It has ‘no tucks or darts’. Unworn, it has ‘a completely flat surface’.[4] p.10 So where does the shape come from?

2Cloth and season

First the cloth. Then the calendar.

Kimono ‘emphasized the material, the fabric of clothing’.[5] PDF p.7 One guide author’s books advise on the ‘suitability and appropriateness of kimono materials, colours, patterns and obi’.[4] p.76

Guide authors remind readers ‘to be aware of the season’ when choosing a pattern.[4] p.62

flower pattern wornreal flower in bloom
Start ‘about one month ahead of the actual blossoming’ and stop ‘just before the flower blooms’.[4] p.62, quoting a 2017 guide

This is advice in guides, not a record of practice. Of her young interviewees Becke writes: ‘Many young women might not be able to recognise this importance’.[4] p.138

3Pattern

A flat cloth is a canvas. Placement is a code.

Flat cloth is ‘an ideal canvas for dyers, painters and embroiderers’.[4] p.11

Komon 小紋: ‘pervasive and repetitive patterns’ are ‘generally considered less formal’.[4] p.62

Eba-moyō: ‘asymmetrical, continuous’ arrangements ‘are regarded as ceremonial’. Motifs gather ‘below the obi and the left shoulder’.[4] p.62Becke reports this from kimono guides and Dalby. The flowers drawn here are decoration; they are not a dictionary of meanings.

4Dressing

The fit is made by dressing, not by cutting.

着付け kitsuke

Flat kimono ‘are fitted to the body as part of the dressing process’. Kitsuke ‘provides the final fit and shape’.[4] p.11

Academies ‘have turned the knowledge and dressing process … into a discipline’, with certificates for finished courses.[4] pp.77–78

  1. firstthe nagajuban (under-kimono)[4] p.72
  2. kimono, ‘draped from the left side over to the right’[4] p.69
  3. obi, with a ‘fold of fabric beneath’ it, the ohashori[4] p.71
  4. obi-jime (obi belt) ‘in the middle of the obi’, and obi-age (obi cloth) ‘to complete the ensemble’[4] p.71
  5. last‘the final tying of the obi knot’[4] p.72

5Movement

Then the rules reach the body.

Furisode magazines teach readers how to move ‘when walking and sitting down, as well as when climbing up and down stairs’.[4] pp.79–80

Also covered: ‘getting in and out of a car’, a meal, the bathroom. The result is ‘constant monitoring and correcting of one’s body’.[4] p.80

Even the collar depends on who sets the rule. Standard furisode dressing ‘aims to hide … the nape’.[4] p.165 In Paris around 1907, a neckband ‘low in back, revealing the nape’ was ‘the fashionable way to wear kimono’.[5] PDF p.8

These are lessons printed for one ceremony, the coming-of-age day. They are not a record of how every kimono wearer moves.

6History

The rules were fixed late.

Before 1868

Fashion, by order

Robes called kosode were the ‘precursor of the modern kimono’.[5] PDF p.4 Laws set ‘the type of clothes members of each class were allowed to wear’.[4] p.99 Pattern books, hinagata-bon, ‘informed consumers’ of new designs.[4] p.28

Meiji · 1868–1912

A national costume

‘Reframed as Japan’s national costume through an encounter with “Western” forms of dress’.[4] p.10 One shape became ‘frozen in time’.[4] p.72

Post-war decades

A rulebook

Japanese dress was ‘largely abandoned by the majority’.[4] p.68 Wearing it became ‘vigorously standardised’.[4] p.11 A 1972 book lists ‘390 rules’.[4] p.75

1990s to now

Rented, revived

‘Rental options gained popularity’.[4] p.118 Then came ‘an increased demand and appreciation of vintage kimono’.[4] p.14

7A test

Take the rules away. Or turn them up.

EuropeWithout the rules

A robe of ease

Long before, kimono ‘had made their way to Holland and become popular as men’s domestic garments’.[5] PDF p.2

Western women saw in kimono ‘an ease and a liberation’.[5] PDF p.4

Fashion found in the kimono a structure for ‘liberation from the corset’.[5] PDF p.11

JapanWith every rule

A robe of form

A magazine calls the furisode ‘the most formal garment for unmarried women’.[4] p.53

Its dressing instructions are ‘communicated as fixed and non-negotiable’.[4] p.72

The rules make it ‘arguably very similar to a school uniform’.[4] p.123

One garment type at one ceremony, not all kimono. Becke adds: ‘Rules can be bended’.[4] p.123

The cut stayed the same.
The meaning did not.

The answerSlide 2, settled

A garment,
or a set of rules?

Front: the garment. ‘A completely flat surface’, cut in straight pieces.[4] p.10

Now turn it over.

Both.

Becke defines kimono by ‘the garment’s shape and practice of fitting it to the body’.[4] p.10 (Bold added.)

‘Seventy percent of what makes a kimono a kimono is how it is worn.’[4] p.11, quoting the head of a kimono house

The garment is simple. The meaning comes from the rules of dressing and formality.
